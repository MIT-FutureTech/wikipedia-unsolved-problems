Wikipedia Unsolved Problems TrackerBeta

A controlled baseline of __N_PROBLEMS__ diverse problems to calibrate the unprecedented progress of AI in mathematics.

AI progress in mathematics has been absolutely extraordinary. In a little over a year, frontier models went from barely achieving gold medals at the International Math Olympiad to a claimed resolution of the Navier–Stokes Millennium problem, one of the holy grails of mathematics.

Given this rapid rate of progress, it's critical to determine if AI is merely conquering “AI shaped” problems, or truly conquering mathematics altogether, replacing the role of human insight in proof generation.

Measuring this is difficult, since frontier labs don't report failures on open problems, only successes. We need a large, diverse set of open problems which are, with high probability, important to their fields. For this, we argue Wikipedia’s public List of unsolved problems in mathematics, snapshotted in January 2024, provides a particularly robust sample.

The dataset is in beta, was generated using LLMs, and has not been reviewed by mathematicians. See About the Data for more limitations and planned adjustments.

Why This Dataset Exists

Lots of great work exists already tracking LLM progress in mathematics. Why introduce another set of problems?

Given the recent breakthroughs on some of the hardest problems in mathematics, more compute is being spent on open problems now than ever before. This introduces two failure modes in the existing literature:

  1. Many existing datasets (like VibeMathed or MathDB) don’t have a fixed list of problems which are causally unrelated to progress in AI. Instead, as more and more mathematicians use these tools, these trackers collect the successful applications comprehensively but have no method of tracking failures. For this, much like in the social sciences, we need a “pre-registered” list of problems to use as comparison, a denominator.
  2. Given the pace of progress on the frontier, even strong existing benchmarks may be near saturation within a few model releases.

Epoch’s FrontierMath benchmarks are the go-to for tracking progress in mathematics, and deliberately mitigate the issue of selection bias by curating a frozen set of challenging problems. These are tremendously strong benchmarks, especially FrontierMath Erdős and FrontierMath Open Problems, due to the effort and expertise invested in curation and formalization (such that they can be verified automatically).

But these datasets suffer from their own modest selection effects: Erdős problems are sampled from the work of a single mathematician, and the Open Problems benchmark is limited to problems with computationally verifiable answers. Depending on the pace of progress, these subsets could quickly become unrepresentative of the true mathematical frontier, as LLMs continue to conquer the most difficult areas of math.

Hence, the ideal question set would be a large set of problems representing the peak difficulty of open problems: diverse enough to avoid selection effects, unsaturated to allow for continued relevance, and fixed in time to avoid polluting our base rates with selection bias. We think Wikipedia’s list of unsolved problems, while far from flawless, captures the necessary conditions nicely: more than 440 problems, across a range of mathematical subfields, notable enough to have a Wikipedia entry (and optionally filtered to have their own Wikipedia page), and fixed in time to a particular point.

Crucially, this is a strong list of problems, which has only become a viable bellwether with the most recent state-of-the-art models. Given the breadth and strength of the list, saturation (or near-saturation) would be extremely strong evidence of AI possessing superhuman mathematics capacity.

How to Read the Data

We mark problems that have a confirmed resolution, a claimed resolution, a partial result, or notable progress. Much of this required judgment calls, by both human and LLM reviewers. See the limitations section for more information.

Resolution confirmed
A proof or counterexample that the community has broadly accepted, or that is machine-verified and unchallenged.
Resolution claimed
A preprint or announcement on the whole problem that experts haven’t yet checked.
Partial result
A listed sub-question or a major special case settled, with the general problem still open.
Notable progress
A new bound or a smaller step; the problem itself is still open.

Two umbrella terms are used throughout this site: “Resolved or claimed” merges “Resolution confirmed” and “Resolution claimed”, and “Progress” merges “Partial result” and “Notable progress”, covering every problem that has moved without being resolved.

Problems with more than one result. Many problems have seen several results since the snapshot: a bound, then a special case, then a claimed proof, sometimes from different people. We keep every one of them, and each chart counts them in its own way:

  • A problem’s status is the furthest it has got: resolution confirmed, then resolution claimed, then progress.
  • A resolution is credited to the first result that settled the whole problem. Its date, its authors, whether AI was involved, and whether it proved or refuted the problem all come from that result. Later resolutions of the same problem are listed beneath it in the Timeline of Resolutions, but they don’t change the credit.
  • Progress is credited across all of a problem’s results.

The research agents recorded any specific reasons to doubt a result, for example a withdrawal, an unanswered error in a key step, or later papers on the same question still calling it open, and the judging pass rated each result’s credibility accordingly. Results rated not credible, withdrawn or shown to contain an error are excluded, and so is any claimed resolution of the whole problem rated doubtful. The one exception is the claims OpenAI published in its own repositories, openai/math (October 2026) and openai/NavierStokesAndEuler (September 2026): they count as claimed resolutions whatever their rating. The releases were weeks old or less when we judged them, and most of the doubts recorded against them were that no expert had yet read it, which is not a reason we apply to any other work. The rating and the reasons for doubt are kept in the data, and claims withdrawn or shown to contain an error are still excluded.

Separately, each result is marked by who did the mathematics, based on the authors’ own account; “AI-involved” covers every category but “No AI”:

AI-generated
An AI system produced the proof, construction or bound, and humans checked it.
AI-assisted
Human-led work in which an AI system produced part of the mathematics in the result (e.g. an argument, a construction, or a case).
No AI
No AI system was used, or it only checked, proofread, searched the literature, debugged code or wrote a formal (Lean) proof of the humans’ argument.
AI and human, separately
Used for progress only. The problem’s results include both work with AI and work without it, in separate results — for example, a bound found by an AI system and a special case proved by mathematicians working on their own.

These categories may need to become more granular in the future, as these tools are used to solve more and more problems.

The Wikipedia List of Unsolved Problems Snapshot

The list of problems is Wikipedia’s List of unsolved problems in mathematics as it stood on 26 January 2024 — the last edit that month, and earlier than every event recorded here. That revision carries 462 bullets across twelve field sections, as well as five Millennium Prize Problems named only in the introduction. Ten of those 467 bullets are headings the article uses to group the problems beneath them, and thirteen name problems that had in fact already been settled before the snapshot (the list had not caught up with them); setting both aside leaves the problems, all open in January 2024, that this tracker counts.

This site includes the option to filter the problems based on whether or not they have (or had, at the time of the snapshot) a dedicated Wikipedia page. This is to help mitigate overinclusion on the list. By default, all problems in the snapshot are included.

You can explore the data and results in Explore All Problems.

Collecting the Data

Every status on this site was last reviewed on __STATUS_CHECKED__.

Every problem was researched on its own by Claude Haiku 5.5 (high effort) with web search enabled, given that one problem and a fixed written protocol. Each agent searched for anything that had happened to its problem since the snapshot, opened the primary sources (arXiv preprints, journal pages, the authors’ own announcements, lab posts, Lean repositories) and recorded every result it found, each with its date, its authors, what it settles, the AI systems involved and supporting links. Agents ran about 40 searches and used about 35 distinct sources per problem on average.

A second pass, Claude Opus 5.5 (low effort), then judged every result separately: how much of the problem it settles (the whole problem, a case or a bound), whether anyone has checked it, whether it is credible, and what role AI played. The statuses and AI credits on this site come from those judgments, not from the research agents.

On 6 October 2026, OpenAI released 749 manuscripts in its openai/math repository, each claiming progress on an open problem. We indexed the repository, used Claude Sonnet 5.5 to match its manuscripts to problems on the list, and used Claude Haiku 5.5 to record what each matched manuscript claims. These were then judged alongside everything else by Claude Opus 5.5.

Two smaller passes added dates: the year each problem was first posed (read from its Wikipedia article where it states one, and otherwise researched in the literature with Claude Haiku 4.5), and the release date of each AI system credited (Claude Haiku 5.5). OpenAI’s internal model has no public release, so it is placed provisionally at GPT-6 Astra’s release.

Next Steps and Limitations

This dataset is a beta version, and there are many improvements left to make. As we make changes, we’ll include versioning to ensure full reproducibility. But our next steps are roughly as follows:

As mentioned above, although Wikipedia tends to be a reliable source, its curation of open problems errs on the side of being overly inclusive. Currently, we control for this with the option to filter problems based on the presence of a dedicated Wikipedia page, but this is still an imperfect proxy of importance. We plan to introduce additional proxies to contextualize these results: total Wikipedia edits, mentions in the scientific literature, subjective LLM or expert ratings of importance.

It is also often hard to tell how much an AI system contributed from the authors’ own account. Disclosures vary widely, from a detailed description of which steps a model produced to a single line thanking it, and a phrase like “played a substantial role” or “used at all stages” doesn’t say whether the model found the key idea or helped a human who did. Where the account is vague, the line between AI-generated and AI-assisted, or between AI-assisted and No AI, is a judgment call, and some results may be classed differently by another reader.
